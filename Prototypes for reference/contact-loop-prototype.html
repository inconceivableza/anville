Notes for implementation

Why this is the cheapest acquisition you have

Every participant recruits five people who already know someone who valued this, have just spent twenty minutes inside the framework, and have demonstrably answered an ask from a friend. They currently get a thank-you and a closed tab. Converting even one in five makes the product self-propagating.

Show their contribution before asking for anything

The first thing they see is their own answers reflected back — the three words they chose, their top picks. It's a genuine thank-you rather than a transition slide, and it costs nothing because it's their own data.

They see their own answers and nothing else. Not the participant's results, not another contact's, and not how many people have replied — response counts are the participant's business, and showing them leaks the shape of someone else's process for no benefit.

The example is clearly marked as an example

What makes the ask persuasive is seeing what the comparison produces — but using the real participant's data would be a serious breach. So the demonstration uses an invented profile, in a dashed border labelled "illustrative — not Ed's results", written in the third person throughout so it can't be mistaken for his. It uses the same visual language as the homepage deliberately: they should recognise it if they go looking.

Be honest about the catch

The comparison requires asking five people of your own, and hiding that until after signup would be a bait and switch on someone who has just done you a favour. The page says it plainly — and turns it into the argument: "you've just been on the other side of that ask, so you know it takes twenty minutes and is oddly enjoyable." Nobody else can make that argument as credibly.

Two touchpoints, not one

The second fires when the person they helped finishes. It's warmer than the first ask and reaches people who weren't ready then. Cap it there — two asks is generous, three is spam, and these are people doing someone a favour.

It must be fully automatic. An earlier version had the participant sending thanks, which adds a task at the exact moment they're finishing something long — so it often wouldn't fire, and the loop would depend on it. The message now says only that they finished, plus a general observation about what people take from it. Nothing is quoted and nothing is asked of the participant.

The card sort is the doorway, not a separate tool

Presenting it as a standalone assessment converts slightly better and then produces a surprise later, which is a bad trade with someone who has just done you a favour. It is framed instead as the first thing on the course: light to begin, honest that there is more, and explicit that stopping is fine. The cost panel carries both numbers — ten minutes to start, three to four hours for the whole thing.

Two ways to pass it on, for two different motives

Not everyone in this moment wants it for themselves. Some immediately think of someone else who needs it, and some think of someone they would like to walk through it. Those are different products:

  • Send it to someone — a plain referral. The recipient owns it; the sender gets nothing back, which is stated so nobody expects updates.
  • Offer to walk someone through it — the recipient starts with this person already named as their coach. Higher value, and it reaches people who would never have been asked otherwise.

The second is worth building carefully. A contact who has just seen the whole mechanic is unusually well placed to coach, and this is the only point in the product where someone volunteers for that role rather than being recruited into it. The invite flow should run them through the coaching commitments before it goes out.

Timings have to match what they actually did

The card sort is about ten minutes; the six written questions add roughly twenty. A contact who did only the sort has a ten-minute reference point, and telling them it takes twenty is both wrong and needlessly off-putting. The copy branches on which they completed, and the cost panel separates the two so the optional half reads as optional.

Data shape

observerInvite {
  id, participantId, contactEmail,
  quantAt, qualAt,
  // loop
  offerShownAt, offerResponse: 'started'|'later'|'declined'|null,
  completionNotifiedAt, secondResponse,
  convertedToParticipantId    // attribution for the loop
}

Track convertedToParticipantId from the outset — it's the only way to measure the viral coefficient, and retrofitting attribution later is painful.

Still to decide

  • Whether contacts who did only the card sort should be asked at all, or nudged to finish the written part first. Currently they get both — a nudge to finish, and the offer.
  • Whether the participant is told which of their contacts signed up. Probably not — it changes who people feel able to ask.
  • Whether the coach invite should require the coaching commitments up front, or let them start and ask later. Up front is more honest; later converts better.
  • Whether anyone who has acted as a contact three or more times should get a different pitch — they may be a natural coach rather than a participant.
