Quarterly check-in

Prototype — the same flow for people whose plan is in the app and people who did it all on paper.

Notes for implementation

Why this is the highest-value thing to build next

The strongest claim on the homepage is that everyone leaves with a practical plan. Nothing currently checks whether anyone does it. This closes that loop, generates the only outcome data that matters to an organisation deciding whether to fund a cohort, and converts a finished course into an ongoing relationship.

Handling people who worked on paper

Offline participants have no roadmap in the system — the app knows their assessment results and nothing about their plan. Two rules make this work:

  • Ask, don't assume. Their first check-in asks what they committed to, in their own words, two or three lines. It takes a minute and it isn't presented as re-entering data they've already written down.
  • Accumulate. Those lines become first-class activities. By the second check-in an offline participant's flow is identical to an online one's, without ever being asked to migrate. That's the graceful on-ramp — most will end up with their plan in the app without having decided to put it there.

The letter is what makes people come back

A check-in that only asks people to account for themselves is a duty. One with something waiting in it is an appointment. The letter written at the end of the course stays sealed until the fourth check-in, roughly twelve months on, and every earlier check-in shows a quiet note saying how long is left.

That note is doing real work: it converts three obligations into a countdown. It costs nothing and it's the single cheapest retention mechanic available here.

The letter is read first, not last

It opens the check-in rather than closing it, because it reframes everything that follows. Answering "did you volunteer somewhere?" hits differently ten seconds after your past self asked whether you'd talk yourself out of it by week three.

Then two questions: how much came true, and what would you say back. The reply matters more than the verdict — it's the most reflective writing anyone does in the whole product, and it costs one textarea.

The letter is always digital, even for offline participants

Everything else in the offline route happens on paper. The letter deliberately doesn't. It's the one guaranteed reason an offline participant returns to the app a year later, which makes it the only reliable channel for the impact data below. Sealing it properly and having it find them on the right date is also something paper can't do.

The flow acknowledges this rather than glossing it: "You did the rest of the course on paper — the letter is the one part we kept here, so it could be sealed properly and find you again a year later."

The four impact questions, asked a third time

The same questions from the first and last day of the course reappear at the twelve-month check-in. This is the most valuable data the product can generate, because end-of-course scores only prove people felt something. Twelve-month scores prove whether it held — which is the question any funder or church leader is actually asking.

Two design points that matter:

  • Asked blind. Previous scores are explicitly withheld until after they answer, and the screen says why: "knowing would change the answer." Anchoring would quietly destroy the data.
  • Placed after the letter. By then they've already reflected honestly on what did and didn't happen, so the ratings are grounded in a year of evidence rather than a mood.

The three-point comparison is then shown on the closing screen, and it's genuinely useful to the individual as well as to you. A decline is handled without flinching: "That is more common than anyone admits, and it is exactly what this check-in is for. Clarity is not a thing you get once."

Writing the next one closes the loop

Having just experienced the value of a year-old letter, people are unusually willing to write another. That turns an eighteen-month course into an indefinite cycle: every letter creates the reason to be there when it opens. It's also the natural point to re-ask the baseline questions in a later version.

Response options are deliberately generous

Four states: done, started, not yet, and no longer the right thing. That fourth one matters — the course teaches that calling clarifies over time, so a plan that hasn't changed in eighteen months may be a worse sign than one that has. Framing a dropped action as discernment rather than failure is both truer and keeps people answering honestly.

The person who did nothing must not feel judged. They're the most likely to disengage entirely and the most worth re-engaging. Asking is itself the intervention — a check-in that gets "no, and I've realised why" is a success.

Unplanned actions are captured separately

Asking "did anything happen that wasn't on the plan?" catches the thing a rigid tracker would miss. Calling emerges through action, and the most significant step someone took this quarter is frequently one they never wrote down.

Drift is a different conversation, not a louder one

After two quarters of no progress the tone changes — not more urgent, but a different question: is the plan wrong, or is the season wrong? Both are legitimate and neither is failure. A nagging check-in gets unsubscribed; one that asks a better question gets answered.

Data shape

checkIn {
  participantId, quarter, mode: 'online'|'offline',
  sentAt, openedAt, completedAt,
  responses: [{ activityId|text, status, note }],
  unplanned: [{ text }],
  carriedForward: [activityId],
  added: [{ text, quarter }],
  coachContact: 'recent'|'awhile'|'none',
  letter: {                      // only on the check-in where it opens
    openedAt, verdict: 'most'|'some'|'little'|'moved',
    reply, newLetterSealedUntil
  },
  impact: { bible, gifts, call, plan }   // 1\u201310, same items as baseline
}

// three comparable points per participant
impactScores { participantId, at: 'start'|'courseEnd'|'month12'|'month24', ... }

letter {
  participantId, writtenAt, sealedUntil, medium: 'digital'|'paper',
  body,                          // null when medium is 'paper'
  openedAt, generation           // 1 = written on the course
}

// offline: `text` entries are promoted to activities
// on save, so the next check-in behaves as online

Still to decide

  • Cadence. Quarterly matches the roadmap, but the first one may be better at six weeks — that's when a plan either takes or doesn't.
  • Whether the coach gets a copy. Strong argument for it, but it changes what people write.
  • Whether an organisation admin sees completion of check-ins, and whether participants are told that.
  • How many unanswered check-ins before stopping. Three feels right; silence is an answer.
  • Whether the impact questions should repeat at 24 months, or whether twelve is the honest limit of attribution.
  • Whether aggregate twelve-month scores are what the organisational waitlist should be shown, rather than end-of-course ones.
  • Whether the letter can be opened early. A "break glass" option respects autonomy and almost nobody uses it, but it does weaken the countdown.
  • What happens if someone never writes a second letter — whether check-ins continue indefinitely without one, or wind down.
