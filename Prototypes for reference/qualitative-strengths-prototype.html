Notes for implementation

Six questions, asked to both sides

The participant and their contacts answer the same six, phrased for each side. Matching sets are what make the comparison work \u2014 every answer has something to sit beside.

QuestionWhy it survives
Most energised and happyObservers often see this more clearly than you do
Three best qualitiesFastest question in the set and the richest to compare
One skill to grow or addMerged from two: people don't reliably sort a skill into "new" or "existing", and the distinction changed nothing downstream
One area of characterKept separate from skill \u2014 they produce genuinely different answers, and the gap between them is often the finding
Biggest change in recent yearsOthers notice change you've stopped seeing
Work you struggle withThe most useful and least comfortable answer

The only question cut outright was "what new skill would open the most exciting doors" \u2014 as a standalone it depended on knowing which doors you want opened, which an observer can only guess at. Folding it into the skill question keeps the intent without the guesswork.

Staged submission

The card sort submits on completion and is never held hostage to the written part. The observer gets a genuine thank-you first, then the second part is offered as an addition. Framing matters: "there's a second part, if you have a little longer" performs very differently from a progress bar that was secretly only half full.

"Send me a link for later" should be a real deferral with one reminder, not a polite decline. Participants see partial data as it arrives, so the comparison is never blocked on the slowest contact.

Making it faster still

  • Three words as three small inputs rather than a textarea — it signals brevity and takes seconds.
  • No mandatory "why" for observers. The participant explains their reasoning; observers just need the observation.
  • Skippable for observers, not for the participant. Partial observer answers are still worth sending — four good ones beat six padded. The participant's own six are the core deliverable of the section, so all are required.
  • Autosave per field. Losing eight minutes of typing is the most expensive failure here.
  • Worth testing: voice input on mobile. These are talking questions, and speaking is three times faster than thumb-typing.

The three-word comparison

This is the centrepiece, and it's effectively a Johari window. Words are normalised (lowercased, stemmed, with a small synonym map) then bucketed three ways: said by both, said only by others, said only by you. Observer words carry a count, because four people independently choosing the same word is a much stronger signal than one.

The middle bucket — what others see that you didn't say — is where the value is. Worth giving it the most visual weight.

Still to decide

  • Whether observers see the participant's answers afterwards. Arguments both ways — it would help the coaching conversation, but it may make people write for an audience.
  • Whether "what do they struggle with" needs a softer frame. It's the highest-value and highest-risk question; anonymity is doing a lot of work.
  • Whether to show the participant how many observers skipped each question — useful signal, or discouraging?
  • Synonym mapping for the word comparison needs a real list. "Kind/caring/compassionate" should probably cluster; "driven/ambitious" maybe not.
