Walking with a coach

This choice matters more than any other you'll make in the course.

The work ahead asks you to be honest about your gifts, your disappointments and what you actually want from your life. That only goes somewhere useful if the person walking alongside you is the right one.

The wrong choice usually isn't a bad person. It's someone who can't resist solving it for you, or who knows you too little to notice when you're avoiding something, or who won't have the time when it matters.

Not what you needSomeone who tells you what they'd do in your position, or who has a view on where you should end up.
What you needSomeone who asks the questions in the material, listens hard, and lets you reach your own conclusions.

We say coach rather than mentor deliberately. A mentor passes on their own experience. A coach draws out yours — which is the harder job, and the one this needs.

Just a first name for now. Six quick questions follow — answer them honestly rather than generously.

Notes for implementation

Why the questions are weighted

Three of the six are treated as non-negotiable: shared faith, trusting their objectivity, and their ability to coach rather than advise. A "no" on any of those ends the flow — no "are you sure?" step, because those aren't things goodwill overcomes.

The other three — how well they know you, their wisdom, their time — are soft. A newer relationship can still work; time can be renegotiated. A single soft "no" produces a considered warning rather than a stop.

Outcome rules

critical = [faith, objectivity, coaching]
soft     = [knowsYou, wisdom, time]

any critical === 'no'        → stop
2+ answers === 'no'          → stop
3+ answers !== 'yes'         → stop
1 soft 'no', or 1–2 unsure   → confirm ("are you sure?")
all yes                      → proceed

Naming the specific concern

The confirm and stop screens list exactly which answers triggered them, each with a line on why that one matters. A generic "you might want to reconsider" gets dismissed; "you weren't sure they'd resist telling you what to do — that's the commonest way this goes wrong" gets thought about.

The mirror

The coach sees the same six commitments written as first-person promises, as tick-boxes. Every box must be ticked to accept. This matters for two reasons: the participant has effectively made claims about this person, and the coach should get the chance to confirm or quietly correct them; and a coach who has explicitly agreed to ask rather than tell is far more likely to do it.

The decline path is deliberately unapologetic — declining is a good outcome, not a failure.

Integration contract

// participant side
const result = evaluateCoach(answers);
// result.verdict  → 'proceed' | 'confirm' | 'stop'
// result.flags    → [{ id, label, why }]

// on proceed (or confirmed), issue an invite
POST /coach-invites { coachName, participantId }
  → { inviteId, url }

// coach side
GET  /coach-invites/:id   → { participantName, commitments }
POST /coach-invites/:id/accept
  { accepted: true, commitments: [ids] }

Why event delegation

All handlers are attached through a single delegated listener using data-act attributes, rather than inline onclick on generated markup. Inline handlers on interpolated HTML break in sandboxed frames and are awkward to test — worth keeping this pattern in production.

Still to decide
